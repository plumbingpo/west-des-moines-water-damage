Wall and Drywall Water Damage: What West Des Moines Homeowners Should Know

Drywall acts like a wick. Once the base of a wall gets wet, moisture climbs through the paper and gypsum, commonly a foot or more higher than the water stood on the floor. From the room, the surface may seem dry and sound even though the hidden face and the insulation are saturated. Looks cannot be trusted here, so every wall gets tested with a meter.

Not every wet wall has to be torn out. Firm panels wetted by clean water are frequently saved, in some cases with the baseboard taken off and small openings drilled so air can move inside the wall. Walls that are mushy, falling apart, moldy or contaminated get a flood cut instead, where our crew cuts straight across above the damage and takes out whatever sits under that line.

What It Looks Like

  • A tide line or discoloration running along the bottom of the wall
  • Fuzzy or dark patches hidden in corners or in back of furniture
  • Baseboards that swell, warp or pull away from the wall
  • Cool, slightly clammy drywall next to walls at normal temperature
  • Bubbling, wrinkled or peeling paint and wallpaper low on the wall
  • Flooring along the wall that is damp, stained or starting to lift
Why Speed Matters

How the Damage Progresses

The first few hours

Water Starts Climbing

Moisture enters where the panel meets the floor and wicks upward from there. Both the baseboard and the bottom plate start absorbing water.

24 to 48 hours

Wall Swells

You may see blistered paint and swollen trim as the damp area moves up the wall. Inside the cavity, the paper face becomes a place mold can begin.

Days 3 through 7

Drywall Breaks Down

Paper peels from a core that is no longer firm. Insulation slumps inside the wall, odor builds and mold spreads across the hidden back side.

Past one week

Damage Reaches the Studs

By this point the studs and plates have been damp long enough for mold and early rot. Much more wall now has to be removed and rebuilt.

What You Cannot See

The Damage You Do Not See

Wet Sheathing Outside

On an exterior wall, water can soak the sheathing behind the insulation. Because it is slow to dry, we test it before any new drywall goes up.

Hidden Behind Cabinets

Drywall hidden in back of cabinetry and built-in units tends to be missed and dries very slowly. Checking under the cabinet base and on the reverse face tells us if it is wet.

Insulation Holding Water

Fiberglass batts soak up water and hold it against the studs. No amount of airflow on the painted side will dry a cavity packed with wet batts.

Wet Outlets and Wiring

Receptacles and cables sit low in the wall, right where water collects. Do not use outlets on a wet wall until the circuit has been checked.

How We Fix It

How We Handle Wall and Drywall Water Damage

1

Map the Moisture

Using meter readings and thermal images, our team outlines where the water went, upward and sideways, on both faces of the wall and in neighboring rooms.

2

Sort Save From Remove

Based on the water category, how long it sat and how the drywall feels, we tell you plainly which sections can be dried in place and which need to come out.

3

Open Up or Air Out

Where the drywall stays, the trim is pulled and airflow is sent inside the wall. On sections coming out, a straight flood cut is made and soaked insulation is removed.

4

Dry and Rebuild

Dehumidifiers and air movers stay on, usually 3 to 5 days, until the studs and the drywall left in place test dry. The rebuild follows with fresh insulation, drywall installation and finishing, trim and a coat of paint.
